The 3 most important factors that make a good plot:
- goal
- stakes
- urgency

Avoid vague and abstract language and prefer specificity.
- Vague language is non-specific/indefinite words, usually words prefixed with "some". Ex: somethings, somewhere. "We spent some time at the park" or "I picked up some things at the store" or "It was somewhat cold outside"
- Abstract language is words with a known definition, but no physical form or sensory value. Ex: ideas, emotions, states, qualities, concepts. (Freedom = idea, sadness = emotion, tired = state, pretty = quality, math = concept)
- Concrete language is something we can experience with the 5 senses

Avoid purple prose. Purple prose is overly embellished language. Something is purple prose when:
- It is convoluted; clarity is lacking or impeded by overwrought style/structure
- Cliche; familiar language and lack of originality
- Melodramatic; intense language not supported by the piece's emotional impact or context

Try to use more interesting and unfamiliar verbs, but not too much and not too crazy. Clarity comes first.

Try to use words that sound like what they mean.

Good descriptions are surprising and insightful.


**Character**

- Give the character a strong voice. A distinct personality that makes the character 3 dimensional
- Avoid all-perfect characters. They must have some flaws or shortcomings to appear more real or human
- No such thing as all-bad or all-good. One dimensional character will just be bad with no purpose
- Character agency: when a character is more proactive than reactive to situations in a story. The best way to check if your characters have agency is to think about replacing them with another character. For example, if you replaced Batman with The Joker, would events still play out in the same way? While the wider plot might stay the same, the individual methods should vary, and the interactions between the characters will be different
- Character goals should have motivation behind them. Both external and internal.
- Internal and external conflicts
- A technique to show emotional resonance without telling is to have a short flashback and of when that character (or different character) lived through something that triggered that same emotion

**Character Arcs**
The main characters must grow, otherwise, they will be stale. Character arcs are usually broken down by
- Exposition: characters are introduced and the setting is established
- Rising action: a series of events leading to the climax that create tension/suspense
- Climax or turning point: when the plot goes from building conflict to resolving conflict
- Falling action: when the main problem of the story is resolved
- Resolution: the solution to the main conflict

**Plot**
- Do not focus too much on the theme. If someone asks "what is your story about?" and you answer with a theme rather than a specific event or character, it's an issue
- The story should follow a plot. There should be specificity. The theme will naturally follow
- Avoid useless scenes. EVERY scene MUST have a purpose. Nobody cares about a character brushing his teeth. Unless brushing them every day will keep demons from destroying mankind, skip it

**Show vs Tell**
- Use adverbs and adjectives sparingly. More often than not, adverbs add no value. Only use adjectives if they add value to the picture
- Use active verbs instead of passive. I.e. "He **_dashed_** to the finish line" vs "He **_was_** running to the finish line" Avoiding "was" and "were" and passive verbs like such
- Don’t explain what is happening in a scene. Let the reader experience it through the character's 5 senses, body language, thoughts, dialogue, etc. **The only exceptions to this rule are:** internal monologue (things the character is thinking), in order to convey a plot point, hint at a future occurrence
- Avoid using filter words (with some exceptions). Example:
	- She saw light beaming through the window = filtered
	- Light beamed through the window = unfiltered

**Dialogue**
- Use dialogue tags sparingly. It is better to use action/movement/expression to convey who's speaking if it's even necessary. Example
	- "It's not my fault," Sarah said
	- "It's not my fault" Sarah crossed her arms.

**Avoid Tropes**
- The chosen one
- Prologues
- The damsel in distress
- The reluctant hero
- Long fantasy names
- Flashbacks (with some exceptions)
- Farmboy to hero (a useless character called into action, similar to the chosen one)

**Book Formatting**
- 8.5 in x 11 in page size
- Size 12 font
- Times New Roman font style
- 1-inch margins
- Double spacing
- One space between sentences
